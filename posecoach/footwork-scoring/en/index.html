The point is won before the swing — look at your feet.

Record a rally. Pose Coach splits your movement into footwork cycles and scores five dimensions — economy, direction, endpoint, stability and efficiency — then tells you the one thing to fix next.

In beta — footwork scoring ships with v1.1. Its thresholds are pre-calibration development values and may change before general availability.

One cycle = from this split step to the next.

Running is scored per step; badminton can't be. Badminton movement is out and back — you split as your opponent hits, move, strike, then recover to base. So Pose Coach uses the moment a split step lands as the cycle boundary: everything between two split steps is one complete footwork cycle.

All five dimensions are computed per cycle, then averaged across the session. The score reflects how well each sortie-and-recovery went — not how much you moved in total.

When the analysis falls back to ② or ③, the "cycle" is a weaker physical unit and the score is correspondingly less interpretable. The card shows which anchor was used.

One footwork score, five weighted dimensions.

Pose Coach reads your footwork cycle data, scores five dimensions 0–100 each, and blends them by weight into a 0–100 overall score and an S/A/B/C/D grade.

A weighted blend of five dimensions. The grade is the overall score mapped to a letter — the same S–D scale that powers our swing and running scores, so grades are comparable across sports.

Weights: Direction 30% · Endpoint 22% · Stability 22% · Economy 15% · Efficiency 11%. Coverage is not part of the score — see below.

The score bands (90 / 80 / 70 / 60) are shared with swing and running-form scoring, so grades are comparable across scores — but the underlying dimensions differ, so the scores themselves are not interchangeable.

The five dimensions

Each dimension answers three questions: what it measures, what a top score looks like, and how to improve it. Ordered by weight, heaviest first — the weighting comes from measured discriminating power, not from which dimension sounds most important.

Direction

Weight 30%

A decisive heading

Measures — whether your displacement within the cycle has a clear heading — a decisive move out and back, versus drifting or changing your mind halfway. The cycle is split into three chunks and their headings compared for consistency.

Top score — all three chunks of the cycle share the same heading: one committed move, no hesitation.

  • Drill six-corner movement and direction changes
  • Give every move a definite start point and end point

This is the heaviest dimension — of the five, it discriminates best between committed movement and hesitation.

Endpoint

Weight 22%

Arriving under control

Measures — whether you arrive under control at the end of a cycle, decelerating into the next split step, rather than still travelling at full speed.

Top score — a controlled arrival you can immediately push off from. Note this is not "coming to a dead stop" — the cycle ends where the next launch begins.

  • Control your centre of mass after the shot
  • Settle before launching the next move

Stability

Weight 22%

Level hips, no bounce

Measures — how much your hips rise and fall while moving. Vertical bounce means energy spent lifting your body instead of moving it where it needs to go.

Top score — hips travelling level through the move, with no up-and-down.

  • Keep knees softly bent through the move
  • Avoid vertical bounce

Not the same as Stability on the swing score. That one measures rhythm consistency across swings; this one measures vertical hip travel while moving.

Economy

Weight 15%

Distance per step

Measures — how much ground each step covers within a cycle. The design intent is that lots of small shuffling steps score low, while efficient shuffles and crossovers that arrive in fewer, larger steps score high.

Top score — arriving in fewer, larger steps rather than many small adjusting ones.

  • Replace shuffling steps with side shuffles and crossovers
  • Cut movement that goes nowhere

Read this one with care — as currently implemented it most directly measures distance covered per step, and that quantity is set by what you recorded as much as by how well you moved. The same player doing a fixed-position drill versus six-corner footwork can differ several-fold on this dimension. Scores from different drill types are not comparable here. This is why it carries the lowest weight.

Badminton strides are far shorter than running strides — this is not "stride length", and it does not compare to the metre-scale strides in our running guides.

Efficiency

Weight 11%

Steps beyond the minimum

Measures — how many steps you took beyond the theoretical minimum for the ground you covered. Anything above that count is a wasted adjusting step.

Top score — reaching the hitting position with no surplus adjustment steps.

  • Read the direction earlier
  • Reach the hitting position in as few steps as possible

Limited spread right now — on current recordings this dimension lands at or near full marks most of the time, and drops sharply on fixed-position drills. Treat a low score as a flag to look at the recording, not as a measured gap in your footwork.

Efficiency is not Economy. Economy asks how far each step took you; Efficiency asks how many steps you spent beyond what was needed.

Coverage

Not scored

Measures — how many footwork cycles were detected — a read on how active you were on court. Genuinely useful (standing and swinging is not the same session as covering the whole court), but it is not a quality metric.

Why it's excluded — moving more isn't moving better. Leaving it in the total would simply reward whoever moved more. It's shown separately, in neutral grey, so the card never reads as six equally weighted bars.

Your score card, element by element

Open the footwork score card and every element is telling you one thing.

  • Score ring + grade badge — the overall 0–100 and its S/A/B/C/D letter with a one-line verdict.
  • One-line summary — names what to fix first. When all five dimensions are already strong, it switches to a "keep it up" line.
  • Five dimension bars — Direction / Endpoint / Stability / Economy / Efficiency, each with its score and a one-line detail.
  • Coverage, set apart — shown below a divider in neutral grey, captioned "not included in the overall score".
  • Tips — up to three, sometimes fewer, sometimes none.
  • Per-cycle strip — each cycle's score, its start and end time, and which split-step type anchored it.
  • Engine version + notice — the scoring engine version, the beta tag, and an honest reminder that scores are directional guidance.

Confidence: with fewer than 2 detected cycles the overall score is capped and flagged as approximate. Play more rallies for the score to mean anything.

Not "work on your footwork" — which part, first

Scoring picks the dimensions that are lagging, ranks them weakest-first, and returns up to three tips — so you fix whatever is dragging the score down most.

EconomyReplace shuffling steps with side shuffles and crossovers; cut movement that goes nowhere
DirectionDrill six-corner movement and direction changes; give every move a definite start and end
EndpointControl your centre of mass after the shot; settle before launching the next move
StabilityKeep knees softly bent through the move; avoid vertical bounce
EfficiencyRead the direction earlier; reach the hitting position in as few steps as possible

Fewer tips is a good sign. Only lagging dimensions generate one. A single weak dimension yields a single tip; when all five are strong you get none at all, and the card tells you to keep it up.

What this score can — and can't — do

Scores reflect AI motion analysis and are directional guidance — not a clinical or professional assessment.

Footwork scoring is in beta and ships with v1.1. Its thresholds are pre-calibration development values, not yet validated against annotated real-player samples — the score is a directional reading, not a footwork-level scale.

It can

  • Split your movement into footwork cycles and score five dimensions per cycle
  • Name the one dimension to work on and give an actionable drill
  • Report how active you were on court, separately from quality
  • Detect split steps and cut cycles at them

Keep in mind

  • Economy currently tracks what you recorded more than how well you moved — scores from different drill types are not comparable on that dimension
  • Step count is estimated from foot-speed peaks, not counted per foot strike
  • Needs your lower body in frame — no legs, no score
  • Needs 20 fps or higher — below that, no score is returned
  • Without split steps it falls back to swing contacts, then to the whole recording, and the "cycle" becomes a weaker unit
  • At most three tips, sometimes fewer or none — no substitute for a coach's live judgement

If your lower body isn't visible or the frame rate is too low, no score is returned and the app asks you to reframe or re-record in-app. Jump and ground-contact detection are not implemented yet — stepping is derived from horizontal foot speed alone.
